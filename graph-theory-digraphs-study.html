Cheat Sheet

  • Graph: \(G=(V,E)\) where \(V\) vertices, \(E\) edges
  • Degree: \(\deg(v)\) = edges incident on \(v\)
  • Handshake: \(\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)=2|E|\)
  • Digraph: directed edges (arcs) \((u,v)\)
  • In/Out-degree: \(\deg^-(v), \deg^+(v)\)
  • Path: sequence of adjacent vertices
  • Cycle: path returning to start

Chapter Overview

Key theme: model structure and flow using vertices and edges, then reason rigorously about connections.

  • Graph theory focuses on relationships rather than geometric size or distance.
  • Digraphs add direction, which captures one-way influence, precedence, and flow.
  • Core invariants (degree counts, paths, cycles) drive proofs and practical algorithms.

1) Graph Fundamentals

Definition: A graph is a pair \(G=(V,E)\), where \(V\) is a set of vertices and \(E\) is a set of edges connecting vertex pairs.

Show Example

Campus shuttle stops can be vertices; a direct shuttle link between two stops is an edge.

💡 Why it matters: Once a problem is mapped to a graph, you can use standard results for connectivity, optimal routing, and resilience.

Degree and Counting

Definition: Degree counts how many edges touch a vertex. In undirected graphs, the total degree sum equals twice the edge count: \(\sum\deg(v)=2|E|\).

Show Solution (Quick Check)

If a graph has 10 edges, the degree sum must be 20. This sanity check is useful when verifying hand-built models.

💡 Why it matters: Degree patterns reveal hubs, isolates, and possible bottlenecks before deeper analysis.

2) Digraphs (Directed Graphs)

Definition: In a digraph, edges are ordered pairs \((u,v)\), so traversal from \(u\) to \(v\) is allowed, but reverse traversal may not be.

Show Example

In prerequisite planning, an arrow from Discrete Math to Algorithms means Discrete Math must be completed first.

💡 Why it matters: Direction is essential for dependency graphs, traffic flow, and influence networks where symmetry is false.

In-degree and Out-degree

Definition: \(\deg^-(v)\) is number of incoming arcs; \(\deg^+(v)\) is outgoing arcs.

Show Example

On social media, follower graph: out-degree = accounts you follow, in-degree = followers you have.

3) Paths, Cycles, and Practical Interpretation

Definition: A path is a sequence of valid edge traversals. A cycle returns to its starting vertex.

Show Example

Supply chain routing uses paths for movement feasibility; a directed cycle in task dependencies can indicate a planning contradiction.

💡 Why it matters: Paths answer “can I get there?”, while cycles often indicate feedback loops, deadlocks, or repeatable processes.
